subtitle: A daily lead brief marked a CRM deal as new when its last-modified date was under a day old. The day after a cleanup, 22 deals were new again, and 19 of them were dead deals from July to September filling 19 of the brief's 25 slots. No script had written to them. All nineteen hung off one contact, the operator's own inbox, and every incoming email on that contact recalculated a hidden rollup on each deal, which re-dated all nineteen within twelve seconds
concepts: last-modified-is-not-last-activity, verify-from-logs, liveness-is-not-correctness
symptom: The operator's 8:00 daily Telegram "Lead Brief" lists CRM deals under "NEW today" when the deal's last-modified timestamp (in HubSpot, hs_lastmodifieddate) is under 24 hours old. The day after a cleanup of stale messages, the brief again showed 22 new deals. Nineteen of them were months-old dead deals, created between July and September 2026, and they took 19 of the brief's 25 slots. No visible field on any of them had changed. The previous evening's re-dating, at 21:08 UTC, had first been blamed on an unidentified job, and the natural next step was to go looking for the cron, bot or agent session that was writing to old deals.
root_cause: Nothing was writing to them. All nineteen deals were associated with one contact record: the operator's own business inbox. An earlier bot had turned emails sent from her own address into deals, so every one of them hung off that single contact. Each incoming email the CRM logs on a contact recalculates a hidden rollup property on every deal associated with that contact, and that recalculation bumps each deal's last-modified timestamp. No visible field changes. The property history records the change with sourceType CALCULATED and zero API writes. Outgoing emails did not propagate this way, only incoming ones. So every email that arrived at her own inbox contact re-dated nineteen dead deals at once, and a brief that read last-modified as "something happened here" put them at the top. The 21:08 UTC bump the day before had the same cause. Three independent read-only probes ruled out every cron, bot and agent session before the email fan-out was found, because the property history showed 0 API writes.
fix: Two changes, one in code and one in data. In code, the brief now ranks and buckets each deal by the deal's OWN activity: the latest of its creation date, the last update to a note on it, and the date it entered its current stage (createdate, notes_last_updated, hs_v2_date_entered_current_stage). It over-fetches 200 deals, ranks them by that date and then slices. The stage-entered date is there because a pre-deploy adversarial review caught a hole in the first draft: a deal moved to a new stage with no new note, which happens when a watcher edits an existing note instead of adding one, would otherwise have looked old. In data, with the operator's approval, the nineteen dead deals were moved to Closed Lost. Nothing was deleted, every note was kept, and each one can be restored in one click. Their Closed Lost Reason carries a prefix that the job hunter's self-learning loop already skips, so a cleanup is never learned as the operator rejecting a job.
verified: Measured live, not inferred. One incoming email was logged on the operator's inbox contact at 14:42:31 UTC. The contact's rollup recalculated at 14:42:45. All nineteen deals were re-dated between 14:42:51 and 14:43:03 UTC, with sourceType CALCULATED and 0 API writes in their property history. After the fix, the rendered next-day brief lists 1 deal as new today instead of 22. The Closed Lost move was checked against the job hunter's learning ledger: it went from 588 to 569 entries, the removed set was exactly those nineteen deals, 0 entries were added, and the learned rules were identical before and after. The nineteen had been sitting in that ledger as false positives, counted as "employer replied", and the replied count fell from 38 to 19. Half of the loop's reply signal had been the operator's own inbox.
rule: A last-modified timestamp is kept by the database for the database. It moves when the system recalculates something, not only when a person acts, so any "recently updated" view built on it measures the database, not the customer. Define activity explicitly, from fields that move only when something you care about happens (created, noted, moved stage), take the latest of them, and before shipping ask what real activity your definition would miss. When many records change in the same second and no field visibly changed, do not hunt for a job first. Read the change history for who wrote it, and look for a shared parent record: one derived field on a parent fans out to every child associated with it. When you clean up the dead records, close and label them rather than delete them, in a way any learning loop downstream can tell apart from a human decision.
