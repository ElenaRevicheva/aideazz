Scoring Hiring Signals on X: When a Lead Generation AI Agent Rejects the Pipeline

November 18, 2026 · by Elena Revicheva

A lead generation AI agent running automated discovery on social signals sounds elegant until your ingestion worker hits 12908 restarts and your downstream qualification filters drop raw signals into the trash. On our host, pm2 jlist monitors 9 processes: 8 online, while algom-stream sits in waiting restart with 12908 restarts on record. Sibling worker algom-poll runs steadily at 79 MB with 0 restarts across 4 days, pulling raw posts, while twitter-api-v2 handles the network layer.

If you build an autonomous scout to ingest posts from X based on hiring-adjacent phrases, you quickly discover that keyword detection is not lead qualification. An automated bot evaluating inbound text from a social feed will happily fill your CRM with noise unless you enforce strict mechanical guardrails.

Here is how our discovery and scoring filters operate, what happens when qualification thresholds trigger outright rejections, and how we measure what enters HubSpot.

Ingestion vs Filtering: The Failure in Unbounded Stream Workers

The architecture separates social stream ingestion from downstream evaluation. In our stack, algom-poll handles scheduled batch polling, consuming 79 MB of memory, while algom-stream was wired to listen to persistent streams. Persistent stream connections fail hard on network flakes or unhandled socket disconnects, leading directly to the 12908 restarts recorded in PM2. Meanwhile, batched polling via algom-poll has sustained 4 days of uptime with 0 restarts.

When social posts match target criteria—such as hiring intent, tech stack shifts, or platform migrations—they are fed into an evaluation pipeline built with @anthropic-ai/sdk and openai. But raw matching creates an immediate operational problem: the volume of superficial matches dwarfs genuinely actionable opportunities.

Our production records show the cost of strict filtering. In apply-queue.log, a single pass processed inbound targets and concluded:

✓ sent to Telegram (1 new)
sunk by kit verdict: 13
✓ sent to Telegram (1 new)

Out of that batch, 13 potential items were sunk immediately by deterministic kit evaluation. Only 1 reached the Telegram notification queue. If an autonomous agent forwards every positive semantic match to your notification boards or CRM, your team spends their day reviewing junk.

Setting Hard Requalification Thresholds: The 25.4% Production Drop

When using bots to qualify business opportunities—whether scraping open requisitions or scoring founder announcements on social media—stale and irrelevant data pollutes the queue.

To prevent human operators from wasting time, our pipeline applies strict requalification jobs. In job-board-watch.log, our validation check evaluated a target stream and emitted this exact log:

FAIL: only 25.4% still live after removing expired and stale postings (need >=40.0%)
VERDICT: REJECTED
RESULT: a wired board FAILED re-qualification (Telegram alert sent)

The system demanded a threshold of at least 40.0% valid items. When the audit measured only 25.4% remaining live, the agent did not attempt to clean up or patch the difference. It executed an automated rejection and notified the operator.

When you configure an AI agent to decide what constitutes a "qualified lead," you must define structural rejection rules that override LLM sentiment. An LLM can always hallucinate a reason why a post looks promising. A hard programmatic check—such as checking whether a referenced URL returns an HTTP 200 or verifying active links—cuts through speculative relevance.

CRM Ingestion Reality: Qualified Deals vs False Signal Accumulation

The downstream destination for vetted opportunities is HubSpot. Raw signals pass through evaluation scripts before any deal creation happens. The state of our live deal pipeline illustrates the bottleneck between high-volume ingestion and genuine prospect replies.

Our deal-digest.log measures the live state:

  • Live job deals tracked: 27

  • Deals in qualifiedtobuy: 16

  • Deals in contractsent: 7

  • Deals in appointmentscheduled: 4

  • Deals in presentationscheduled: 0

  • Deals in 14-day window: 7

Looking across broader HubSpot totals recorded in the digest:

  • qualifiedtobuy: 16

  • contractsent: 130

  • decisionmakerboughtin: 80

  • appointmentscheduled: 1531

Across our HubSpot deal search API, deals specifically marked at "They replied" (dealstage=contractsent) stand at 136.

Notice the distribution: 1531 opportunities reached scheduled stages historically, but active, verified deals in qualifiedtobuy sit at exactly 16. That ratio is not an accident. When automated agents source leads, top-of-funnel capture is trivial. Converting unstructured text matches into a clean stage of 16 requires dropping the vast majority of signals before they ever reach a salesperson's eyes.

Multi-Agent Coordination Without Memory Leaks

Managing this pipeline across our team involves two autonomous coding assistants: Cursor and Claude Code. As documented in our operator queue file /home/ubuntu/cto-aipa/docs/oracle/NOW.md:

"Cursor Cloud, Cursor Desktop and Claude Code all work this repo and none of them can see each other's chats. No shared conversation, no Claude MCP in Cursor, no way to send the other agent a message. The only things all of them read are HubSpot and this..."

To keep operations from stepping on each other across multiple commits—we recorded 12 commits in cto-aipa and 4 commits in aideazz over the last 48 hours—the agents rely on explicit file-level tracking and shared logs.

In concierge-selftest.log, our automated verification verifies duplicate suppression across incoming cards:

ok   Telegram card delivered (then removed) — removed=true
ok   duplicate suppressed (redundant drafters collapse) — {"ok":true,"duplicate":true,"existing":"a9c31d3cfa848781"}
✅ PASS — 5 checks, 4306ms to first card

Running 5 checks in 4306ms ensures that duplicate signals picked up from social monitoring or job boards collapse into an existing hash rather than generating parallel outreach tasks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Why separate the stream listener from the polling worker if both target the same data source?
A: Persistent stream connections can fail repeatedly under network churn, causing algom-stream to record 12908 restarts in PM2. Running algom-poll as an isolated worker allowed it to maintain 0 restarts over 4 days with a predictable 79 MB memory profile.

Q: What automated rule determines when an ingested signal stream is dropped?
A: Our requalification script drops any feed where live valid items fall below a strict threshold; in our production log, a feed showing 25.4% active postings was rejected because the requirement was at least 40.0%.

Q: How many active opportunities survive filtering to reach the qualified stage in CRM?
A: In our HubSpot deal digest, 16 deals sit in qualifiedtobuy out of 27 live job deals tracked, while total historical records show 136 deals reached the "They replied" stage.

Q: How do disconnected AI coding agents prevent duplicate outreach drafts?
A: Deduping occurs via payload hashing; our self-test verified duplicate suppression across 5 checks in 4306ms, ensuring identical signals collapse into existing thread identifiers.
